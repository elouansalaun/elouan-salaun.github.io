02 — Markowitz

A constrained optimizer, and the reason not to trust it

The whole frontier rests on one scipy.optimize.minimize call with SLSQP. minimize_vol minimizes √(wᵀΣw) under two equality constraints — weights sum to 1, and portfolio return equals a target — with bounds (0, 1) on every weight, which is how the repo forbids short selling. optimal_weights sweeps a grid of target returns between er.min() and er.max() and traces the curve.

Two portfolios on that curve are found without a target return. msr maximizes the Sharpe ratio by minimizing its negative. gmv is the neat trick of the course: msr(0, np.repeat(1, n), cov) — feed the optimizer identical expected returns for every asset, and since it can no longer improve the Sharpe ratio through the numerator, it can only lower volatility. The global minimum variance portfolio falls out of the max-Sharpe solver, and it never touches an expected-return estimate.

Estimation error is amplified, not absorbed

Notebook 8 is the demolition. Take two industries whose true annualized returns over the window are almost identical — Food at 11.68%, Steel at 11.58% — and perturb the estimates by a point or two. The optimizer does not spread the error; it goes to a corner.

The same pathology shows up at 30 assets: the tangency portfolio holds only 6 of them, 46% of it in Hlth; the GMV holds 10, 48% in Util. Both look precise and are neither diversified nor stable.

Notebook 9 closes the argument from the other side. Rolling 36-month average pairwise correlation across the 30 industries is negatively correlated with the trailing 36-month market return: when the market falls, correlations rise and the diversification you optimized for evaporates exactly when it is needed.

What the repo concludes

Two escapes are proposed, both consisting of estimating less. The equally weighted portfolio requires no estimation at all and sits well inside the frontier but survives contact with the future. The GMV requires only a covariance matrix — a far more stable object than expected returns. Neither is optimal ex ante; both are defensible ex post.

03 — The pivot

Cash is not the risk-free asset

Measured in absolute value, cash never loses. Measured as a funding ratio — assets over the present value of the liability — it collapses as soon as rates fall, because the liability's present value rises. The genuinely risk-free asset becomes the one whose price moves like the liability: a zero-coupon bond of matching maturity, approximated in practice by a bond portfolio of matching Macaulay duration.

04 — Monte Carlo

Two generators, and a distribution instead of an average

Simulation is what lets the repo test strategies that have no closed form. Two processes feed everything downstream.

GBM (gbm) draws (n_steps, n_scenarios) normal shocks in one numpy call and cumulative-products them into price paths — no Python loop. Returns are drawn already in 1+R form, and the first row is forced to 1 so every path starts at s_0.

CIR (cir) is the interest rate side: dr = a(b − r)dt + σ√r dW. The mean-reversion term pulls rates back to b, and the √r factor shuts volatility down as rates approach zero, which is what keeps them positive. Because the model works in the instantaneous short rate, ann_to_inst / inst_to_ann (log1p / expm1) convert at both ends. The same function also returns the analytic zero-coupon price P = A(t,T)e−B(t,T)r — so one call gives you both the rate path and the price of the hedging asset.

The discretization trap

Notebook 11 spends real effort on one line. Drawing per-step returns centred on mu*dt + 1 looks right and is not: over 10 years at μ = 7%, terminal prices average 200.4 against a theoretical 196.7, a consistent upward bias of nearly 2%. Centring on (1+mu)**dt instead gives 196.2. Compounding is multiplicative; the drift has to be too. It is the kind of error a simulation will never flag on its own.

Why the average is the wrong summary

The repo makes a point of not collapsing 5,000 scenarios into one number. terminal_stats reports the mean and standard deviation of terminal wealth, but also p_breach (probability of finishing below a floor) and e_short (expected shortfall conditional on breaching). You live one path, not the average of five thousand — so the shape of the distribution is the result, and the mean is a footnote.

05 — CPPI

The feedback loop that turns a wish into a guarantee

Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance is the one genuinely dynamic algorithm in the repo, and everything in notebooks 10 through 18 is a variation on it. At each step: compute the cushion, the fraction of account value sitting above the floor; invest a multiple m of that cushion in the risky asset, clipped to [0, 1]; put the rest in the safe asset; let the market move; repeat. Because the cushion shrinks as you approach the floor, the risky weight goes to zero before the floor is hit — the protection is mechanical, not predictive.

Notebook 10 also makes a performance point in passing: this is one of the rare places where a Python for loop is unavoidable, because each step depends on the previous account value. Everything else in the toolkit vectorizes; this cannot.

The multiplier is the whole risk decision

m sets how aggressively the cushion is levered, and its reciprocal is the tolerance: 1/m is the largest single-period drop the strategy can absorb before the floor is breached. At m = 3 that is a 33% monthly loss — comfortable. At m = 10 it is 10%, and rebalancing monthly is no longer often enough. This is gap risk, and it is visible in the numbers.

The allocator as an argument

The cleanest piece of engineering in the repo is that bt_mix(r1, r2, allocator, **kwargs) knows nothing about the strategy. It receives a function that returns a T×N weight matrix, then computes w·r1 + (1−w)·r2. Adding a strategy means writing an allocator, not touching the backtester. Only the allocators that read the account state back can guarantee anything.

The result the whole repo builds toward

The fixed mix and the glidepath buy risk reduction with return, and never fully eliminate failure — 70/30 gives up 0.21 of mean wealth and still breaches on 0.8% of paths. The floor allocator eliminates every breach while giving up 0.03. It can do this because it knows the price of the hedging asset at every instant, so it always knows exactly how much cushion it has; the other two allocate blind.

On real data

Simulation is not proof, so notebook 18 ends on the reconstructed cap-weighted market index. A drawdown-constrained CPPI holds its constraint and improves risk-adjusted return, at a real cost in raw return.

Worth noting what does not work: a fixed floor at 80% of the initial stake changes nothing over this period. Wealth grew more than tenfold, so the cushion stayed saturated and the risky weight stayed at 100% throughout — max drawdown unchanged at −50%. An absolute floor only protects the first few years, which is precisely why the ratcheting and discounted floors exist.

06 — Code notes

Things to know before reusing the module

  • String comparison with is — filetype is "returns" in get_ind_file relies on string interning; SyntaxWarning on Python 3.8+.
  • "floor" defined twice in run_cppi's return dict — the scalar parameter is overwritten by the floor history and can no longer be read back.
  • plot_ef returns the axis only when show_gmv=True — the return ax sits inside the conditional block.
  • Mixed units on the frontier — annualized returns plotted against a monthly covariance matrix.
  • Hard-coded "data/…" paths — the module only works from the repository root; Path(__file__).parent would fix it.
  • sns.distplot and ipywidgets 7 — notebooks 12, 17 and 18 will not run as-is on a current environment.
