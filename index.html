Public procurement is about 13% of GDP across OECD countries, and in guarding and patrol services almost every contract is won or lost through a call for tenders. Yet the bid itself is usually priced from memory: a margin that worked on a job that felt comparable, corrected by instinct for a competitor someone met last year. The paradox is that the market publishes nearly everything needed to do better, specifications, opening results, awards, amendments, and almost none of it is used.

This is my MSc thesis, carried out at Polytechnique Montréal, in collaboration with a leading provider of security services in Québec. It is one system, not five experiments: a scraper that finds the tender, a retrieval pipeline that reads its PDF, a similarity model that finds what the company already knows about contracts like it, a ranking model that proposes a margin, and an interface a bid manager actually opens. Each module is only as useful as the one feeding it, which is the whole difficulty of building it end to end.

01 — Architecture

Five modules, one database

The framework is deliberately built around a single store rather than a pipeline of scripts. Modules 1 and 2 write to it, modules 3 and 4 read from it and write back their derived objects, and module 5 only reads. That constraint is what allows a tender scraped on Monday to be scored, compared and priced on Tuesday without anybody exporting a spreadsheet.

02 — Monitoring

Recognising a contract you have already priced once

Tenders are published on SEAO, the Québec government electronic tendering system: specifications, submission forms, contractual clauses, addenda. Open Canada gives the publication URLs, scraping gives the rest — descriptions, bid opening results, awards, amendments. Collected over January 2015 to March 2026, that is 1,960 publications, with award information available for 94% of them: who bid, at what price, and who won.

The useful part is not the list, it is the link. A tender that is a renewal of a contract the company already holds, or already lost, is the single most informative prior available at bid time. Nothing in the data says so explicitly, so the match is made on descriptive attributes with sequence-based entity resolution (SequenceMatcher, Levenshtein distance) across client names, sites and descriptions that are never written the same way twice. Over 2025 the process retrieved 60 tenders with 92% concordance, enough to turn the dashboard into an early-warning list rather than a news feed.

03 — Reading the documents

A retrieval pipeline shaped like the contracts it reads

What actually drives cost in a guarding contract is buried in prose: how many uniforms of which type, what equipment, whether a patrol vehicle is required and who supplies it. These are sentences in a PDF, spread across sections that vary in position from one client to the next.

Because the contracts follow highly regular formats, PyMuPDF can label headers, paragraphs and lists structurally, and that structure is kept: chunks are built hierarchically, each one carrying the section it belongs to instead of being a flat 500-token window. Retrieval then filters by level before scoring, so a query about vehicles is answered from the operational section rather than from a clause that merely mentions the word.

Retrieval is sparse, BM25 over vectors enriched with a security-domain vocabulary, and that is a deliberate choice over dense embeddings. The vocabulary here is narrow, technical and exact; the difference between two uniform types is one adjective, and lexical matching does not blur it. The retrieved chunks go into a prompt carrying explicit extraction rules and a JSON output schema, and Ministral-3-14B-instruct returns the structured record.

From words to a number that can be compared

Extracted entities are matched against a hand-built cost dictionary using spaCy, exactly where possible and partially otherwise, then aggregated per category. A contract stops being a PDF and becomes three numbers: what its uniform, equipment and vehicle requirements are worth. The validation is indirect but convincing, the distribution of uniform scores lands on the price points the company actually charges.

04 — Similarity

Teaching a distance what "comparable" means to a bid manager

Every contract is described by operational, financial and contextual variables: region, client type, duration, price, workload, resource requirements, and the attributes coming out of the RAG pipeline. Continuous variables are min-max scaled, and each variable type gets the comparison it deserves, categorical similarity matrices, sequence comparison on names, Euclidean distance on the numeric block, a temporal decay so a 2016 contract counts less than a 2024 one, Jaccard on sets of requirements. The individual distances are then aggregated into a single score.

The aggregation weights are the only place where judgment enters, so they are the thing to learn. An expert was asked, over 300 iterations, to pick and order the three most comparable contracts out of the top 15 returned by the distance score. Note what is being collected: rankings, not ratings. Experts are unreliable when asked how similar two contracts are on a scale, and very reliable when asked which of two is closer.

A small neural network (3, 2), initialised with equal weights, is then refined on that base with a RankNet pairwise objective, trained on 80% of the expert comparisons and tested on the remaining 20%. It improves both the exact-trio ratio and the mean Spearman rank correlation over the hand-weighted distance, the model ends up agreeing with the expert about ordering, which is what the pricing module downstream consumes.

Why this module carries the whole system

Module 4 never sees a raw contract. It sees the set of past contracts that module 3 declares comparable, and the winning margins attached to them. If the similarity is wrong, the pricing model is confidently answering the wrong question, which is the reason the expert base exists at all, and the reason the evaluation is done on held-out human rankings rather than on the internal loss.

05 — Pricing

Rank the margins, do not regress them

The decision at the end of the chain is one number: the markup ratio to apply. Two things bear on it, the technical characteristics of this contract and the state of the market right now, and the model is built to hold both. Similar contracts come from module 3, their winning markups are re-indexed to today using market evolution indicators derived from rolling means of past winning ratios, and recent tender results are added on top to capture short-term competitive behaviour.

The modelling choice is the interesting part. With only a few exploitable tenders, a regression on the winning margin is both data-hungry and mis-specified: it optimises squared error, while the business loss is violently asymmetric. A margin one point too high loses the contract entirely; a margin one point too low wins it and leaves money on the table. So the problem is reformulated as Learning to Rank: an XGBRanker with a pairwise objective, evaluated with NDCG, orders a grid of candidate markups from 1.05 to 3.00. The target is not a value but a position, the highest candidate that still wins.

What is measured

A bid is not evaluated on its distance to the winning price alone, because a bid that is always close and never first earns nothing. Six metrics are tracked, and they deliberately disagree with each other.

The result the whole module turns on

Regression wins the distance metrics, MD 0.250 against 0.280, and loses the money, 1.59 M$ against 3.52 M$. It is not a contradiction, it is the mechanism: to minimise absolute error against an unknown winning margin, the safest move is to shade every bid downwards, which converts wins into thin wins. The LTR model takes exactly the same 40% win rate and prices those wins 62% above the reference in use. Being close to the winning margin is not the objective; being just under it is.

A second run on 57 tenders, with chronological training sets growing from 31 to 117 contracts, checks that this is not an artefact of one test window: the model ranks first on 33 tenders (59%) and within the top three on 83%, with only two badly underestimated bids.

06 — Interface

The part that decides whether any of it is used

All of the above reaches bid managers through a single Streamlit application with two entry points. The first is monitoring: ongoing tenders, upcoming renewals, and a one-click path from an opportunity into its analysis. The second is the contract view, where technical characteristics can be entered or loaded and the models answer the historical contract this one renews, its outcome and applied margin, the comparable contracts retrieved by module 3, and links back to the original procurement sources so that every number can be traced.

The market overview is the view that changed conversations the most: historical and market-adjusted margins by contract group, and competitor profiling by participation rate, win rate and pricing behaviour. A bid meeting that used to open on "what did we charge last time" now opens on what the market charged, who showed up, and where the margin has drifted since.

Limits

What this does not yet establish

The evaluation is retrospective. Every result above answers "what would this have bid, had it existed", on 54 to 57 exploitable tenders, a small dataset by any standard, and one where a handful of large contracts move the dollar figures. A retrospective win is also a simplification: it assumes competitors would have bid the same way against a different price, which they would not. And the pricing recommendations have not yet been validated prospectively by the experts who would have to sign them.

The natural continuation is a deployment with a feedback loop, so each new opening result retrains the ranker; enrichment of the historical base; extension beyond guarding to other service sectors; and the step the framework makes visible but does not take, optimising a portfolio of simultaneous bids rather than each tender on its own.

Quebec publishes everything about its public procurement. Every call for tenders, every bid amount, every award and every amendment is released as open data in the Open Contracting Data Standard, weekly, since 2020. It is also, as published, close to unusable: multi-gigabyte JSON dumps of deeply nested releases, which is an excellent archive format and a terrible way to answer a question.

And the questions are simple ones. Who holds this buyer's security contract today, and until when. What does guarding actually go for in Montreal. Does this buyer rotate suppliers or renew the same one. How much of the market do the top three firms hold. Each is three joins and one modelling decision away from the raw feed, and it is the modelling decision, never the joins, that quietly returns a plausible wrong number.

So this repository is two things. A three-script pipeline that turns the feed into a query-ready SQLite file, and a read-only MCP server that hands a language model a tool surface over it. The point is not to give a model SQL access, a generic SQLite MCP server would do that in one line. It is to give it the correct SQL, frozen into tools, for the handful of questions where the obvious query is wrong.

01 — Architecture

Three scripts to build, one server to serve

The build side is deliberately three separate scripts writing three separate artifacts, rather than one pipeline object. Each stage is idempotent and can be re-run alone: the fetcher compares the feed's updated timestamp against local file mtimes and downloads only what moved, the filter is a pure function of the dumps on disk, and the normalizer rebuilds the database from scratch every time. Refreshing the data next month is three commands, not a migration.

Nothing in DATA/ is committed. The weekly dumps are several gigabytes and the database is a derived artifact, so the repository ships the recipe and not the result.

What gets kept

The filter is the one place where the scope of the whole project is decided, and it is eight lines at the bottom of build_raw_json_db.py: a release is kept if its title matches one of eight French keywords ( gardiennage, agents de sécurité, service de surveillance, patrouill and so on ) and does not mention incendie, or if its first item carries one of six UNSPSC codes in the public-safety family. Releases are then deduplicated on ocid, since a process reappears in every weekly dump it was touched in. Point those two lists at another sector and the rest of the repository is unchanged.

02 — Schema

OCDS is nested, questions are relational

An OCDS release is one deeply nested object per contracting process: parties, tender, items, lots, bids, awards, contracts, amendments and payment transactions all hanging off a single ocid. Normalizing it is mostly patient work, the source mixes NEQ/neq, totalAmount/totalamount, durationInDay/durationInDays, so every field goes through a pick() helper that takes the first present case variant, but two structural decisions carry the whole design.

The first: party ids are release-scoped. The OP-387 in one process is not the OP-387 in another, so every party-keyed table carries the ocid and every join is on the pair (ocid, party_id). The second: the only identifier that is stable across processes is the NEQ, Quebec's enterprise number, which is why it gets its own index and why every aggregate in the server keys on it.

Two things the database carries for the model, not for me

An FTS5 index built with tokenize = 'unicode61 remove_diacritics 2', aggregating per process the title, the buyer, the supplier names, the item descriptions and every party name. French procurement text is written with and without accents in the same corpus; this makes securite and sécurité the same token, so the model never has to guess the spelling.

And a data dictionary inside the database itself, a schema_doc table of table/column/description rows. The server's describe_schema reads it and hands the model the same notes a new analyst would be given, that value_unit is a SEAO code and not a currency, that municipal is a flag, that value_total_amount is the full-term figure. The documentation travels with the artifact instead of living in a README the model will never read.

03 — Why not a generic SQL server

Three traps that return a plausible wrong number

The tempting answer to all of this is to point an off-the-shelf SQLite MCP server at the file and let the model write its own SQL. It works, in the sense that it always returns a table. Three properties of this data make that table wrong often enough to be worse than useless, and none of the three announce themselves: the query succeeds, the number looks reasonable, and it is off by a factor.

The three modelling decisions the curated tools take out of the model's hands.

04 — Tool surface

A flexible core, and six answers already worked out

The surface is deliberately hybrid. Six curated tools cover the questions worth getting right, with the modelling baked in and nothing left for the model to decide; three flexible tools cover everything else. describe_schema is the map, search_processes is the usual entry point, and run_sql is the escape hatch for the long tail, the questions I did not anticipate, which are most of them.

What the curated tools return is not rows. It is a short report: amounts formatted in dollars, region codes expanded to names (6 → Montreal), bid unit codes translated to what they mean, percentiles rather than raw distributions, and a closing line stating what was excluded and why. The tool answers in the analyst's vocabulary so the model does not have to reconstruct it from integers.

objective 1
Evaluating a tender you are about to bid on

incumbent(buyer, service?) returns every award for that buyer, most recent first, supplier, awarded value, signed contract value, contract end. price_benchmark(unspsc?, text?, area?) gives the awarded-value distribution and, for competitive tenders only, the winning bid distribution and the winner-versus-next-lowest-rival margin.

objective 2
Knowing who you are bidding against

supplier_profile(name) resolves the firm to its NEQs and reports win rate over open and limited tenders, total won, method mix, top buyers, areas targeted and the rivals it meets most often. head_to_head(a, b) counts the tenders where both submitted, and who took each one, including the both-won-different-lots case.

objective 3
Reading a buyer

buyer_profile(name) reports open versus gré à gré mix, total spend, distinct supplier count and the top supplier's share of value, over 50% is flagged as loyal, below as rotating, with awarded volume by year.

objective 4
Sizing the market

market_overview(year?, unspsc?, top_n?) gives total awarded value by year, the share held by the top-N firms as a consolidation measure, the procurement-method trend and the busiest delivery areas.

05 — Guardrails

Read-only by construction, not by instruction

A tool that executes model-written SQL is a tool that executes model-written SQL. Asking politely for SELECT only is not a control, so run_sql is fenced at four independent levels and none of them depends on the model behaving.

  • The connection cannot write. Every call opens file:…/seao.db?mode=ro through the SQLite URI interface. A write is refused by SQLite itself, whatever passed the parser.
  • One statement, one verb. The query must start with SELECT or WITH, may not contain a semicolon, and is matched against a whole-word blocklist of twelve verbs, insert, drop, attach, pragma, vacuum and the rest — so stacked statements have nowhere to go.
  • Bounded results. A LIMIT is appended when the model omits one, and the caller's limit is itself clamped to 2,000 rows. An unbounded scan cannot flood the context window.
  • Bounded time. Each query runs under a threading.Timer that fires conn.interrupt after five seconds. A pathological join is cancelled rather than hanging the client.
  • No shared state. A fresh connection per call, which keeps things thread-safe under FastMCP's worker pool and costs nothing on a database this size.

The same logic runs in reverse for correctness: test_mcp_tools.py calls all nine tools with the README's own example questions against the built database, and includes a DELETE that is expected to be refused. It is a smoke test rather than a suite, but it is the one that catches a broken build before a model has to discover it mid-conversation.

06 — Running it

Local by default, remote if you insist

The server speaks stdio and finds its database at DATA/seao.db next to the script, overridable with SEAO_DB_PATH. In Claude Code a project-scoped .mcp.json is committed, so opening the repository is enough to be prompted to enable it; Claude Desktop takes the same command and arguments in its own config file.

ChatGPT is the awkward one. It only connects to remote MCP servers over HTTP or SSE and cannot spawn a local process, so reaching it means wrapping the stdio server in mcp-proxy and exposing that through a tunnel. It works, and the README walks through it, but it is worth being honest about what it costs.

What the tunnel actually does

For as long as it runs, a local database is reachable from the internet with no authentication. The server is read-only and the underlying data is public, so the exposure is bounded, but it is real, and the right posture is to keep the tunnel up only while a conversation needs it. Locally over stdio, none of this applies: nothing listens on a port.

Source data is Quebec's SEAO open data, published through open.canada.ca in OCDS format; the code is MIT-licensed, the data stays under its publisher's terms. Coverage skews to 2021–2025 and the subset is security services only, so figures the server returns are indicative rather than exhaustive.

02 — Markowitz

A constrained optimizer, and the reason not to trust it

The whole frontier rests on one scipy.optimize.minimize call with SLSQP. minimize_vol minimizes √(wᵀΣw) under two equality constraints, weights sum to 1, and portfolio return equals a target, with bounds (0, 1) on every weight, which is how the repo forbids short selling. optimal_weights sweeps a grid of target returns between er.min() and er.max() and traces the curve.

Two portfolios on that curve are found without a target return. msr maximizes the Sharpe ratio by minimizing its negative. gmv is the neat trick : msr(0, np.repeat(1, n), cov) feed the optimizer identical expected returns for every asset, and since it can no longer improve the Sharpe ratio through the numerator, it can only lower volatility. The global minimum variance portfolio falls out of the max-Sharpe solver, and it never touches an expected-return estimate.

Estimation error is amplified, not absorbed

Notebook 8 is the demolition. Take two industries whose true annualized returns over the window are almost identical, Food at 11.68%, Steel at 11.58%, and perturb the estimates by a point or two. The optimizer does not spread the error; it goes to a corner.

The same pathology shows up at 30 assets: the tangency portfolio holds only 6 of them, 46% of it in Hlth; the GMV holds 10, 48% in Util. Both look precise and are neither diversified nor stable.

Notebook 9 closes the argument from the other side. Rolling 36-month average pairwise correlation across the 30 industries is negatively correlated with the trailing 36-month market return: when the market falls, correlations rise and the diversification you optimized for evaporates exactly when it is needed.

What the repo concludes

Two escapes are proposed, both consisting of estimating less. The equally weighted portfolio requires no estimation at all and sits well inside the frontier but survives contact with the future. The GMV requires only a covariance matrix, a far more stable object than expected returns. Neither is optimal ex ante; both are defensible ex post.

03 — The pivot

Cash is not the risk-free asset

Measured in absolute value, cash never loses. Measured as a funding ratio, assets over the present value of the liability, it collapses as soon as rates fall, because the liability's present value rises. The genuinely risk-free asset becomes the one whose price moves like the liability: a zero-coupon bond of matching maturity, approximated in practice by a bond portfolio of matching Macaulay duration.

04 — Monte Carlo

Two generators, and a distribution instead of an average

Simulation is what lets the repo test strategies that have no closed form. Two processes feed everything downstream.

GBM (gbm) draws (n_steps, n_scenarios) normal shocks in one numpy call and cumulative-products them into price paths, no Python loop. Returns are drawn already in 1+R form, and the first row is forced to 1 so every path starts at s_0.

CIR (cir) is the interest rate side: dr = a(b − r)dt + σ√r dW. The mean-reversion term pulls rates back to b, and the √r factor shuts volatility down as rates approach zero, which is what keeps them positive. Because the model works in the instantaneous short rate, ann_to_inst / inst_to_ann (log1p / expm1) convert at both ends. The same function also returns the analytic zero-coupon price P = A(t,T)e−B(t,T)r, so one call gives you both the rate path and the price of the hedging asset.

The discretization trap

Notebook 11 spends real effort on one line. Drawing per-step returns centred on mu*dt + 1 looks right and is not: over 10 years at μ = 7%, terminal prices average 200.4 against a theoretical 196.7, a consistent upward bias of nearly 2%. Centring on (1+mu)**dt instead gives 196.2. Compounding is multiplicative; the drift has to be too. It is the kind of error a simulation will never flag on its own.

Why the average is the wrong summary

The repo makes a point of not collapsing 5,000 scenarios into one number. terminal_stats reports the mean and standard deviation of terminal wealth, but also p_breach (probability of finishing below a floor) and e_short (expected shortfall conditional on breaching). You live one path, not the average of five thousand, so the shape of the distribution is the result, and the mean is a footnote.

05 — CPPI

The feedback loop that turns a wish into a guarantee

Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance is the one genuinely dynamic algorithm in the repo, and everything in notebooks 10 through 18 is a variation on it. At each step: compute the cushion, the fraction of account value sitting above the floor; invest a multiple m of that cushion in the risky asset, clipped to [0, 1]; put the rest in the safe asset; let the market move; repeat. Because the cushion shrinks as you approach the floor, the risky weight goes to zero before the floor is hit, the protection is mechanical, not predictive.

Notebook 10 also makes a performance point in passing: this is one of the rare places where a Python for loop is unavoidable, because each step depends on the previous account value. Everything else in the toolkit vectorizes; this cannot.

The multiplier is the whole risk decision

m sets how aggressively the cushion is levered, and its reciprocal is the tolerance: 1/m is the largest single-period drop the strategy can absorb before the floor is breached. At m = 3 that is a 33% monthly loss, comfortable. At m = 10 it is 10%, and rebalancing monthly is no longer often enough. This is gap risk, and it is visible in the numbers.

The result the whole repo builds toward

The fixed mix and the glidepath buy risk reduction with return, and never fully eliminate failure, 70/30 gives up 0.21 of mean wealth and still breaches on 0.8% of paths. The floor allocator eliminates every breach while giving up 0.03. It can do this because it knows the price of the hedging asset at every instant, so it always knows exactly how much cushion it has; the other two allocate blind.

Worth noting what does not work: a fixed floor at 80% of the initial stake changes nothing over this period. Wealth grew more than tenfold, so the cushion stayed saturated and the risky weight stayed at 100% throughout, max drawdown unchanged at −50%. An absolute floor only protects the first few years, which is precisely why the ratcheting and discounted floors exist.
